# People Signed Up for My News App. Almost Nobody Paid.

People were signing up for Trace.

They completed onboarding. They chose their interests. They reached the feed.

Then almost nobody paid.

My first reaction was to search for one big explanation.

Maybe the positioning was wrong. Maybe the price was wrong. Maybe technology news was not painful enough for people to pay for. Maybe the product did not feel premium. Maybe I had built something people liked but did not need.

All of those were possible.

The analytics appeared to confirm the problem: visitors became signups, most signups completed onboarding, and then the funnel collapsed at the subscription step.

It looked like a pricing problem.

It was not that simple.

## My funnel was mixing two different products

Trace exists on mobile and the web.

The mobile app is free to use. The web product has a subscription path.

I was looking at both populations inside one conversion funnel.

That made the headline numbers almost meaningless.

A mobile user could sign up, complete onboarding, use the product exactly as intended, and never subscribe because the mobile product did not ask them to. In the combined dashboard, that perfectly healthy journey looked like failed conversion.

The mobile app naturally produced a better signup rate too. People who install an app have already shown more intent than someone who lands on a website from a search result.

Putting those users in the same funnel did not create a useful average. It erased the distinction I needed to understand.

This sounds obvious in hindsight. It was not obvious while watching the numbers move.

A dashboard can be mathematically correct and still describe a product that does not exist.

There was no single Trace funnel. There was a free mobile journey and a paid web journey sharing some of the same events.

Before changing the product, I needed to separate them.

## The paywall was asking people to buy a blank page

Once I isolated the web experience, a more embarrassing problem became visible.

The paywall was clean. It had a headline, a short list of benefits, and a button.

It also sat in the middle of an almost empty page.

Someone arriving there could read that Trace included technology discussions, trending projects, top blogs, and filtered social signals. What they could not do was see any of it.

The product was asking for trust before providing evidence.

“Full summaries and context” sounds useful. It is not as convincing as seeing an actual summary.

“A feed built around your interests” sounds personalized. It is not as convincing as recognizing three stories you would have opened that morning.

“Get the signal without the scroll” sounds good in a headline. It does not tell someone what tomorrow’s briefing will look like after they enter a card number.

I had treated the paywall as a pricing component.

For a content product, the paywall is part of the content experience. It has to answer a much more basic question:

**What exactly will I get after I pay?**

Mine did not.

## The feed was hiding its own value

The subscribed feed had the opposite problem.

There was plenty of content, but too much of it was squeezed into small cards and narrow columns.

Titles ended halfway through. Descriptions disappeared after a few lines. Hacker News and GitHub items were compressed into a side rail. Social posts competed with deeper reads. Some cards looked neat until I tried to understand why the story mattered.

I was showing users that Trace had collected the content without letting the content make its case.

That distinction matters.

A news product is not valuable because it contains 20 links. The browser already contains an unlimited number of links.

The value is in selection, context, grouping, and the confidence that the reader can stop afterward.

When every description is truncated, context becomes a promise instead of something the reader experiences. When five sources cover the same event separately, collection looks like noise. When a card is too small for the title, the interface communicates that fitting the grid mattered more than the information.

The design was making the product look thinner than it was.

## I was optimizing the sale before proving the habit

Subscription products often talk about the paywall as the moment of conversion.

That is technically true and strategically incomplete.

The real conversion begins earlier, when a user recognizes that the product can replace an existing habit.

For Trace, that habit might be checking Hacker News, scanning X, opening GitHub Trending, and saving several long reads for later.

If the free experience does not demonstrate that replacement, the paywall has to sell an abstract benefit. Abstract benefits are easy to agree with and easy to leave.

People already know they spend too much time scrolling. They do not need another product to explain information overload to them.

They need to experience one morning where the product makes the routine easier.

That changed how I think about what belongs before the paywall.

The goal is not to give away a crippled interface and reserve all visible value for subscribers. The goal is to let someone understand the shape and quality of the paid experience while keeping the ongoing utility paid.

A useful preview could include:

- A real daily edition, not placeholder cards.
- Complete titles and enough context to judge the editorial quality.
- A visible example of duplicate coverage grouped into one storyline.
- A few personalized sections based on onboarding choices.
- Clear boundaries around what remains locked and what a subscription keeps delivering.

The preview should feel like the product. Otherwise the paywall is advertising something the user has never met.

## “Premium” was not a visual style

I initially associated premium with polish: restrained colors, large type, generous spacing, and fewer interface elements.

Those things help. They are not the reason someone pays.

Premium, in this product, means that the decisions feel expensive.

Why is this story here?

Why was that source chosen?

Why are these four links grouped together?

Why did the product show me this discussion but ignore 50 louder ones?

Why can I read the full thought instead of a chopped paragraph?

Those are editorial and product decisions. A beautiful card cannot compensate for weak selection. A minimalist paywall cannot compensate for hidden value.

The interface should make the decisions legible.

## The honest ending is that I do not have the conversion chart yet

This is not one of those founder posts where changing a button produced a 240 percent increase in revenue by Friday.

I do not know yet which change will have the largest effect.

Separating mobile and web analytics will make the diagnosis honest. Showing the product before asking for payment should make the offer clearer. Giving important content enough room should make the feed more useful. Shorter summaries for fast-moving sources should keep the page readable.

Those are improvements to the system. They are not proof of demand.

Users may still decide that a daily technology briefing is nice to have but not worth paying for. If that happens after they have clearly experienced the product, it will be valuable evidence about the market or pricing.

If they leave after seeing an empty modal, it tells me almost nothing.

That is the main lesson I am taking from this:

**Do not interpret a broken explanation of value as a rejection of the value itself.**

Fix the measurement. Show the real product. Let people understand the trade. Then believe the result.

I am still doing that work with [Trace](https://www.yourtrace.online), a personalized technology briefing built from top reads, community discussions, projects, and the topics each reader follows.

If you are building a subscription product, look at your paywall and ask one uncomfortable question:

Could a person explain what they will receive tomorrow, using only what they can see today?

If not, the problem may not be your price.

They may simply have no idea what you are selling.
